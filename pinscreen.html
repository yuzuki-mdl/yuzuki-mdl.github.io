the pinscreen

a screen of a million pins, raked with light. push them in, the shadows draw the picture.

in 1935 alexandre alexeieff patented a machine with no ink and no film in the usual sense: a white screen pierced by hundreds of thousands of steel pins, each able to slide in and out a little. rake a light across it from the side and every pin casts a tiny shadow. the deeper the pin, the longer the shadow. the picture is made of nothing but relief and grazing light.

he and claire parker made whole films on it — night on bald mountain (1933), the nose (1963). each frame was pushed by hand, pin by pin, then raked and shot. it is probably the slowest animation technique ever devised.

drag on the screen to push pins in. the canvas simulates a small patch of alexeieff's screen: each cell is a pin, its brightness is its depth, and the shading comes from a light raking across the surface at the angle below. tilt the light and the same relief reads completely differently — which was exactly the point.
