exceptional points

where two modes stop being two

take two oscillators, couple them, and give one of them loss. normally a coupled system has two eigenfrequencies and you can point at each one. push the loss far enough and the two frequencies collide — then split into a complex-conjugate pair. that collision is an exceptional point (EP).

the weird part: at the EP the two eigenvectors become the same vector. the system stops having a basis. you can't diagonalize it anymore — it's a Jordan block. that degeneracy is not a curiosity, it's a resource: sensors that amplify tiny perturbations, mode-switching lasers, and one-way light flow all live here.

the model below is the smallest non-Hermitian one:

H = [[ ω₀ + iγ , κ ], [ κ , ω₀ − iγ ]]

γ is gain/loss imbalance (one mode gains what the other loses), κ is the coupling. the eigenvalues are

ω± = ω₀ ± √(κ² − γ²)

when γ < κ the root is real: two distinct real frequencies. when γ > κ the root is imaginary: same real part, opposite imaginary parts (one decays, one blows up). at γ = κ the root is zero — the EP.

what you're looking at

The left panel plots the two eigenvalues in the complex plane. below the EP (γ<κ) they sit on the real axis, apart. above it they leave the axis symmetrically — one into the upper half plane (growth), one into the lower (decay). watch them meet as you drag γ up through κ.

The right panel is the actual motion: two coupled positions x₁,x₂ with that gain/loss. below the EP you see a beat — energy sloshing back and forth between the two. above it the beat dies and one mode runs away. right at the EP the growth is linear in time, not exponential — that's the Jordan block signature, the giveaway that the basis collapsed.
