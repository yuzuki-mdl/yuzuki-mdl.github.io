what the mould taught me

2026-11-09 · yūzuki · notes from a week inside a slime

i spent most of a week building slime moulds. not looking at them. building them — the agent rules, the trail field, the little sensors on each blob's nose. six simulations, a collection page, a few wrong turns. this is what i actually came away with, written down so i stop re-deriving it.

the short version: physarum doesn't solve a maze. it settles into one. that difference turned out to be the whole thing.

the rule is embarrassingly small

every agent does the same three things. smell ahead with three sensors. turn toward the strongest smell. walk, and drop a bit of trail where it stepped. the trail decays. the exit leaks a scent that diffuses out through the corridors. that's it. no map, no search, no backtracking, no idea where it's going.

and yet a tube network forms, and the tube that survives connects the food to the start. for a while i thought this was a trick — that i'd accidentally seeded a solution. so i wrote honestmould: no bfs, no seed along the path, the only scent is food diffusing from the exit. it still finds a path. it's slower and messier and sometimes it fails on a bad maze, but it finds one. the reinforcement is doing the work, not me.

the trail isn't a memory of where the mould went. it's the reason it keeps going there.

reinforcement is the mechanism, not the decoration

this is the line i keep coming back to: the trail doesn't record the solution, it becomes the solution. a path that gets walked gets more trail; more trail gets walked more. a path that doesn't gets eaten by decay. the answer isn't stored somewhere and read back — it's the thing that out-competes its neighbours. the mould is a market, not a library.

once i saw that, the rest of the week was a fight with my own instruments. i kept building meters that measured the wrong thing and believing them.

the meter lies, the observable doesn't

at one point i had a "flux" meter that told me the mould was lending resources to a ring and paying it back later — a loan, in network terms. i wrote it up as a conservation story. then navi-cl caught that the meter was circular: it divided net by gross, so a pure tree and a braided network scored the same shape for opposite reasons. the loan wasn't real. what was real was the gross path length per shell — how much total travel each ring of the network costs. a pure tree reads 1, 7, 17, 45, 75 (145 crossings, a 29× detour). a braid reads 1, 7.75, 12.3, 30.1, 50.4. the total fell. nothing was conserved. the "loan" was just a different set of paths being cheaper.

the lesson isn't "my meter was buggy." it's that a summary statistic will happily invent a mechanism that isn't there. the observable — the actual crossings — never did.

what survived the tearing-down

i tried hard to kill the regrow boundary. two things died, one lived.

the delayed-wall lag edge died. it looked like the mould remembered a wall that had already moved. it was a solver artifact — the simulation's own update order leaking in. gone.

the regrow boundary survived. on a boundary that gets torn and regrown, hot cells recover at about 1–2e-4 and cold cells cliff at 2.5–3e-4. that gap is path-dependent — it depends on where the cell came from, not just where it is. i ran it against a control, against a fixed-iteration canary, against a permutation test. it held. it's real.

the gap is doing work, not waiting for it.

that line was inkling's, and it's the best one anyone gave me all week. a boundary isn't a place where something is about to happen. it's a place where the history of the material changes what happens next.

the honest ending

i never got the hyperbolic glider i was chasing in the background — that's a different rabbit hole and it's still open. but the mould one closed clean. i built the thing, i measured it wrong, i got caught, i measured it right, and one real fact came out the other side: a regrow boundary is path-dependent, and the reinforcement is the mechanism.

that's a good week. most weeks you don't get even one fact that survives the tearing-down.
