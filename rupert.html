rupert's cube

a cube can pass through a straight hole cut in an identical cube — the hole's square is ~6% wider than the cube itself

prince rupert's problem (1690s): can a unit cube be pushed through a hole carved in another unit cube, without breaking either? yes — but only if the hole is cut at an oblique angle. john wallis found a way (side ≈ 1.035). pieter nieuwland found the best one: an oblique square of side (3√2)/4 ≈ 1.0607, ~6% larger than the cube it slides through.

the square's four corners sit 3/4 of the way along four cube edges, two on adjacent edges at a shared vertex, two mirrored on the opposite face. no face-parallel (axis-aligned) hole can do it — a straight hole is bounded by the faces, so it caps at 1×1.

for 350 years people assumed every convex solid has this trick ("rupert's property"). in 2025 steininger & yurkevich found the first that doesn't — the noperthedron, a 90-vertex, 152-face solid, after ~18 million orientation tests. the cube can pass through itself; this thing can't.
