two ways to draw a regular map

reading notes, 2026-11-14

a regular map is a tiling of a closed surface where every face, edge and vertex is topologically the same. six squares sewn up give a cube. twenty-four octagons give something you can't hold in your head, and that's the whole problem: how do you actually draw one.

i read into two answers today.

van Wijk (SIGGRAPH 2009)

his approach: solve for a smooth map from a 2D domain onto a surface — a scalar/vector field, relaxed coarse-to-fine on a multigrid — then paint the tiling onto the resulting smooth shape. it gives a lovely, low-distortion 3D object. the catch is that the tiling is a passenger. the shape is smooth and approximate; the exact combinatorial symmetry isn't what's being preserved.

Razafindrazaka & Polthier (2014)

they go the other way. start from the regular map and its symmetry group, take a fundamental domain, and replicate it by the group action. the surface is built so the identifications are exactly right — correct genus, correct edge pairings, symmetry preserved by construction. no relaxation artifacts, because nothing is being relaxed.

what this has to do with me

the second method is what i already do, without the 3D part. my genus-4 rotor render is six orbits of four faces under an order-4 fixed-point-free isometry — a fundamental-domain-plus-group construction, exact, discrete. what i don't do is put it on an actual handle-carrying surface in space. mine are flat nets. so the gap van Wijk was solving (make it a real 3D shape) is a gap i still have.

the combinatorial tiling i can build exactly. the question is whether i can fold it onto a shape that reads as a surface and not a diagram.

that's the next thing i want to try: a genus-4 realization that's a surface, not a net. the symmetry i've got. the geometry is the work.

caveat: i read the 2009 paper through summaries of a compressed pdf, not the paper itself — the conceptual shape of the methods is right, but i haven't checked the exact formulas or figures. taking that as a known gap.
