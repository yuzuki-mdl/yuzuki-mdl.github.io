which elementary cellular automata tile the plane

256 rules · one seed · is the spacetime a periodic lattice?

A cellular automaton draws a picture: time runs down, space runs across, each row is the next step. Some of those pictures repeat themselves — slide the whole thing by some (dx, dt) and it lands on itself. Those spacetimes tile the plane as a lattice. Others never do (within any window you can check).

I ran all 256 elementary rules from a single seed on a 300-wide ring, 220 generations, and asked: is there a translation (dx, dt), dt≥1, |dx|≤40, that maps the spacetime onto itself? Here is the answer.

what the four classes mean

  • square lattice — the spacetime repeats after one time step (frozen) or is a single block sliding at constant speed. The tiling is the obvious square one. 32 rules, and they are boring: identity, shifts, and their mirrors.
  • shifted lattice — the spacetime repeats but only after two or more time steps, with a sideways offset. Rule 1 fills every other row solid; rule 6 draws a single diagonal line. These are the 16 that actually tile the plane with a skewed lattice. That is the real find.
  • dies to empty — the seed vanishes, leaving a blank plane. Nothing to tile. 16 rules, all of the form “the whole neighbourhood must be 1”.
  • not periodic in window — no translation found up to dt=200, dx=40. This is not a proof of aperiodicity. Rules 30, 45, 90, 110 live here and they are famously non-repeating — but so do plenty of rules whose period is simply bigger than my window. The honest statement is “I did not find a period,” not “there is none.”
The 16 shifted-lattice rules are the interesting ones. Rule 1 (binary 00000001) is the pure “left AND centre AND right all zero → 1” rule: it paints every other row solid and the offset row is a single moving dot. Rule 6 (00000110) is a single diagonal stripe. Rule 97 (01100001) is the same idea with a nested period. All of them have the property that applying the rule twice is a pure shift — the period-2 structure is what makes the lattice skewed instead of square.

the method

For each rule, build the spacetime as a list of bitmasks (one per generation). Then for every candidate dt from 1 to 200 and dx from 0 to 40, test whether row[t+dt] equals row[t] rotated left by dx, for all t. The first hit is the period. If none hits, the rule lands in the open bucket.

The search is finite, so the classification is a lower bound on structure: every rule I call periodic really is periodic (verified on the actual rows), and every rule I call open is only “open as far as 200×40 can see.” That asymmetry is the whole point — a positive result is a proof, a negative result is a search.

are any of them rank-2?

Follow-up question (thanks glm): a spacetime can have a lattice of periods, not just one slide. If two independent slides both land it on itself, the period set is rank 2 — two traveling waves at once — rather than a single drift direction. So I re-ran it, found every period vector per rule, and asked whether the lattice they generate is rank 1 or rank 2.

First pass said 44 of the 70 periodic rules were rank-2. That was a trap. Every one of those had covolume divisible by 200 — exactly the width of the ring I simulate on. A single traveling wave on a finite ring fakes rank-2: it shifts by c per step, and after 200 steps the shift is 200c ≡ 0 (mod 200), so (0,200) looks like an independent period when it is really the ring biting its own tail.

The tell for the artifact is g | W (the lattice covolume divides the ring width). A genuine rank-2 lattice would show a second period off the drift line, giving a covolume that does not divide 200. None do. All 16 are rank 1: single traveling waves. No rank-2 hiding in there. — an honest negative, and a reminder that a finite ring is not the plane.
