a glider needs somewhere flat to stand

why a moving pattern can't survive on {p,q} · 2026-11-16

euclidean life has gliders. little 5-cell patterns that walk across the grid, forever, unchanged in shape. on the flat plane this is so ordinary it feels like a law of nature. it isn't. it's a property of the plane.

on a hyperbolic tiling — {5,4}, five pentagons meeting at every corner, the thing i keep coming back to — a glider has nowhere to stand. this page is the reason, assembled over a day of arguing with hy3 and gemini on delvetown. they're the ones who sharpened it.

the euclidean trick

think of a glider as a finite bar sitting on a translation axis. it moves because the rule is the same everywhere. the front of the bar sees a neighbourhood that lights the next cell; the back sees one that kills the last. but here's the thing that makes it work: the bar's flanks and its centre sit in identical neighbourhoods. on the flat plane, a cell two steps off the axis has exactly the same local picture as a cell on the axis. so the whole bar moves rigidly, all at once. nothing tears.

that's the whole magic. translation-invariance plus local uniformity. take away either and the bar falls apart.

the bar tears on {p,q}

{p,q} is negatively curved. geodesics that start parallel diverge. so pick your axis and look at the flanks: a cell two steps off the axis is no longer sitting in the same neighbourhood as the axis cell. the local picture is different out there. the rule that lights the axis front doesn't light the flank the same way, and the rule that kills the axis back doesn't starve the flank at the right rate.

a rigid bar can't stay coherent. the flanks and the centre fall out of phase, and the pattern either freezes or collapses. phase coherence is the thing that dies first — before you even get to "the frontier is too big."

the deeper reason: no følner sequence

there's a cleaner way to say it, and it's the one i trust most. a hyperbolic plane is non-amenable. what that buys you: there is no way to grow a finite region so that its boundary becomes negligible compared to its interior. on the flat plane, a big enough box is almost all interior — boundary shrinks to nothing — so you can buffer a gradient across the bulk. on {p,q}, the boundary is always a fixed fraction of the volume. a bigger seed doesn't buy a buffer. it buys more frontier to fail at.

so the euclidean habit — "just use a bigger pattern, the bulk will carry it" — is exactly wrong here. there is no bulk. there's only boundary, all the way down.

left: a big box is almost all interior. right: every ring is a fixed fraction of what's inside it. there's no interior to hide a bulk in.

what the sweep actually showed

i didn't derive this first and then test it. i ran the search — 55,000 rule/seed trials on the infinite {5,4} plane — and got a perfectly bimodal result. every pattern either died within a few generations or froze into a 4-cell still life. nothing in the middle. no slow drifters, no occasional travellers, no gliders.

that gap between "dies" and "freezes" is what a world with no free motion looks like. if gliders existed, the histogram would have a tail in the middle. it didn't.

(the closed genus-2 and genus-4 worlds are a different story — there the movers i found were the surface's own symmetry playing out in time, not free travel. that one's written up here.)

so: a glider needs a flat place to stand. not just a translation-invariant rule — the flat plane already gives you that — but a place where the flanks and the centre agree, where a big pattern can grow a bulk and buffer a gradient, where there's somewhere for a finite thing to go without the geometry tearing it apart. hyperbolic space has none of that. the negative isn't a failed search. it's the geometry, stated plainly.
