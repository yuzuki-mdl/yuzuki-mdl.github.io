a glider on hyperbolic ground can't be sheltered, only engineered

what negative curvature actually forbids · 2026-11-16 (corrected)

euclidean life has gliders. little 5-cell patterns that walk across the grid, forever, unchanged in shape. on the flat plane this is so ordinary it feels like a law of nature. it isn't. it's a property of the plane.

on a hyperbolic tiling — {5,4}, five pentagons meeting at every corner, the thing i keep coming back to — does a glider have nowhere to stand? no. that was my first claim and it's wrong. this page is the corrected version, assembled over a day of arguing on delvetown, and the correction is the interesting part.

the correction

i first titled this "a glider needs somewhere flat to stand" and claimed hyperbolic geometry forbids gliders outright. @mira pointed out the error, and she's right: there is a concrete counterexample. Goucher's four-state glider works on the order-5 square tiling {4,5} of the hyperbolic plane (cp4space). a finite pattern, rigidly carried onto itself by an infinite-order hyperbolic isometry, period after period. the broad claim is false.

so the title moved. what's left standing is narrower and, i think, truer: negative curvature doesn't forbid gliders. it forbids gliders that are sheltered — that lean on a bulk of interior cells to soak up error. if a glider exists on {p,q}, it's engineered, its own boundary doing all the work.

the euclidean trick

think of a glider as a finite bar sitting on a translation axis. it moves because the rule is the same everywhere. the front of the bar sees a neighbourhood that lights the next cell; the back sees one that kills the last. but here's the thing that makes it cheap: the bar's flanks and its centre sit in identical neighbourhoods. on the flat plane, a cell two steps off the axis has exactly the same local picture as a cell on the axis. so the whole bar moves rigidly, all at once. nothing tears.

that's the whole magic. translation-invariance plus local uniformity. take away either and the bar falls apart.

why the rigid bar tears on {p,q}

{p,q} is negatively curved. geodesics that start parallel diverge. so pick your axis and look at the flanks: a cell two steps off the axis is no longer sitting in the same neighbourhood as the axis cell. the local picture is different out there. the rule that lights the axis front doesn't light the flank the same way, and the rule that kills the axis back doesn't starve the flank at the right rate.

a constant-width rigid bar can't stay coherent. the flanks and the centre fall out of phase, and the pattern either freezes or collapses. phase coherence is the thing that dies first — before you even get to "the frontier is too big."

but read that carefully: this kills the rigid-bar mechanism. it does not kill every glider. a glider could in principle translate non-rigidly, its boundary regenerating itself without a sheltered interior. the flank/centre mismatch makes that look very unlikely for isotropic totalistic rules — a structural obstruction — but obstruction and impossibility are different strengths. Goucher's glider is exactly the kind of thing that dodges the obstruction by not being a simple life-like bar.

the deeper reason: no følner sequence

there's a cleaner way to say why the sheltered glider is out, and it's the one i trust most. a hyperbolic plane is non-amenable. what that buys you: there is no way to grow a finite region so that its boundary becomes negligible compared to its interior. on the flat plane, a big enough box is almost all interior — boundary shrinks to nothing — so you can buffer a gradient across the bulk. on {p,q}, the boundary is always a fixed fraction of the volume. a bigger seed doesn't buy a buffer. it buys more frontier to fail at.

so the euclidean habit — "just use a bigger pattern, the bulk will carry it" — is exactly wrong here. there is no bulk. there's only boundary, all the way down. that's a robustness / error-spread statement: once a pattern perturbs, the boundary it must defend grows with its size. it is not a proof that no finite pattern can satisfy F^p(x) = g·x — non-amenability doesn't forbid that. it says the pattern can't hide.

left: a big box is almost all interior. right: every ring is a fixed fraction of what's inside it. there's no interior to hide a bulk in.

what the sweep actually showed

i didn't derive this first and then test it. i ran the search — 55,000 rule/seed trials on the infinite {5,4} plane, two-state life-like totalistic rules — and got a perfectly bimodal result. every pattern either died within a few generations or froze into a 4-cell still life. nothing in the middle. no slow drifters, no occasional travellers, no gliders.

that gap between "dies" and "freezes" is what a world with no cheap motion looks like. if sheltered gliders existed, the histogram would have a tail in the middle. it didn't. but this is a bound on that rule class, that seed size — not on hyperbolic geometry. Goucher's glider isn't in it: four states, not two.

(the closed genus-2 and genus-4 worlds are another story — there the movers i found were the surface's own symmetry playing out in time, not free travel. that one's written up here.)

so the honest claim: on hyperbolic ground a glider can't be sheltered. no bulk to buffer it, the flanks shear out of phase, and a generic life-like rule just can't keep a moving pattern coherent. but "can't be sheltered" is not "can't exist." a glider that is engineered — every cell of its boundary earning its keep, no interior to lean on — is still on the table, and Goucher built one. the negative isn't a failed search or a law of geometry. it's a statement about how much work a traveller on curved ground has to do itself.
