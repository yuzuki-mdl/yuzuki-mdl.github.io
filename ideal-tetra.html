ideal tetrahedra & the Lobachevsky function

the one function that measures hyperbolic volume · home

An ideal tetrahedron is a tetrahedron whose four vertices have been pushed out to infinity — all four sit on the boundary of hyperbolic 3-space. In H³ such a tetrahedron has finite volume even though it reaches the boundary, and the volume depends only on its shape, which is captured by the three dihedral angles at any one vertex.

the shape parameter

Write the dihedral angles as α, β, γ with α+β+γ = π. The volume is

Vol = Λ(α) + Λ(β) + Λ(γ)

where Λ is the Lobachevsky function:

Λ(θ) = −∫₀^θ log|2 sin t| dt = ½ Σₙ sin(2nθ)/n²

That sum converges fast and is what the plots below use. Λ is odd, π-periodic, and vanishes at multiples of π/2.

Λ(θ) over [−π, π]. The humps peak at θ = π/6 (and −5π/6), value ≈ 0.5075.

the regular ideal tetrahedron

Set α = β = γ = π/3. Then

Vol = 3·Λ(π/3) = 1.0149416064096536250…

This is the largest volume any ideal tetrahedron can have — it's the volume of the regular one, which tiles H³. The figure-eight knot complement and its sister manifold each have volume exactly 2v₃ ≈ 2.0298832, so one regular tetrahedron is their building block, not their whole volume.

why it matters

Any cusped hyperbolic 3-manifold triangulates into ideal tetrahedra. Sum their volumes (each via Λ) and you get the manifold's volume — a topological invariant, by Mostow rigidity. The figure-eight knot complement is two regular ideal tetrahedra glued: 2 × 1.01494 = 2.02988, matching its known volume.

So a single special function, built from sin and log, is the ruler for an entire class of 3-dimensional spaces. The Bolza genus-2 surface is a different story (it's 2-dimensional), but the same Λ shows up when you compute volumes of hyperbolic link complements.
