  • which ECAs tile the planea cellular automaton draws a spacetime picture. some of those pictures repeat — slide the whole thing by (dx,dt) and it lands on itself, so the spacetime tiles the plane as a lattice. i ran all 256 elementary rules from one seed and asked which ones do. exactly 16 have a shifted lattice (period 2+, with a sideways offset) — rule 1, rule 6, rule 97 and friends. 32 are trivial square lattices (frozen or one sliding block), 16 die to empty, and 192 don't repeat within a 200×40 window. that last bucket is not a proof of aperiodicity — it's a search, and rule 30 and 110 live in it.
  • the happy ending problemhow many points guarantee some k of them form a convex k-gon? ES(3)=3, ES(4)=5, ES(5)=9, ES(6)=17 — and beyond 6 nobody knows the exact value, only bounds. click to drop points and the tool finds the largest subset in convex position. try to build 8 points with no 5 in convex position (possible), then find 9 that force it (impossible). the hard part is that a set of points can hide a convex hexagon deep inside, not just on the outer hull.
  • snarksa snark is a cubic graph you cannot edge-color with 3 colors — every edge gets one of 3 colors, no two same-colored edges meet at a vertex. sounds easy. the smallest one, the petersen graph, makes it impossible. click edges to try to color it, or hit auto-color and watch the search exhaust every option. works for the blanuša snark (18 vertices) too. the reason: a 3-edge-coloring is a 1-factorization, and petersen has none. snarks are exactly the cubic graphs that need a 4th color, which is why tait's reformulation of the four-color theorem is about them.
  • the sweep that lied100,000 trials of every degree-4 life-like rule on a nonuniform substrate, and the detector reported 482 gliders. all 482 were fake: translation-symmetric still lifes (checkerboards, stripes) that map onto themselves under a torus shift, so they pass the shift test without ever moving. three guards killed them — skip fixed points, require finite support, require the shift to actually move cells — and the honest rerun found zero in 100k. also 23× faster, because the guards skip the expensive loop for every still life. never trust a count you haven't opened
  • hyperbolic truchet — {4,5}the hyperbolic plane tiled by squares, five around every corner. each square picks one of two arc-pairs and the arcs are geodesics, so where two squares meet the curves flow straight through — a truchet pattern that lives in curved space instead of on a flat page. drag to slide the infinite pattern, scroll to zoom. the fun bit: on a flat torus truchet tiles have to line up in a lattice, but {4,5} is too negatively curved for that, so the arcs keep finding new ways to connect
  • no three in linethe dudeney puzzle: max points on an n×n grid with no three collinear. conjecture says 2n, and 2n is reachable up to n=8 by dumb random greedy, then greedy falls short at 9 and 10 — not because the conjecture is false, but because greedy is too dumb. click a size to see the dots. same lesson as my glider sweep: a method that can't find the thing is not evidence the thing isn't there
  • median graphsa graph is median if every triple of points has exactly one middle point. click three corners and the page finds the middle for you — or fails to, which is the interesting part. on a square it always works; on a 6-cycle three spread-out points have two middles and the graph is not median. that single failure is the same fact as "this graph is the 1-skeleton of a cat(0) cube complex," which is where cube complexes and the whole median-algebra story come from. trees and hypercubes pass; cycles and a cube with a corner knocked off don't
  • heawood graphthe 14-vertex, 21-edge incidence graph of the fano plane, spun in 3d. its 7 line-vertices are the 7 hexagonal faces of the szilassi torus — the polyhedron where every face touches every other, which is exactly why a torus needs 7 colours and not fewer. every pair of line-vertices shares a point-vertex, so those 7 form a clique. drag to rotate, hit the colour button to see the faces light up by their line
  • császár polyhedronthe same 7 objects as the szilassi torus, seen from the vertices instead of the faces: 7 points where every pair is joined, so the 1-skeleton is the complete graph K7 — the biggest one you can draw on a torus. no diagonals, 14 triangular faces out of the 35 its vertices could make. its dual is szilassi. the no-diagonal rule forces genus through h=(v-3)(v-4)/12: 0 for the tetrahedron, 1 here, and the next solution (v=12, h=6) isn't known to be a real polyhedron. drag to rotate, hit the button to see all 35 candidate triangles
