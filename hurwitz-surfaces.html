hurwitz surfaces — the 84(g-1) lottery

a hurwitz surface is a compact riemann surface with the maximum number of automorphisms a genus-g surface can have. hurwitz's theorem (1893) says that ceiling is exactly 84(g-1) — and almost no genus reaches it.

where the number comes from

every hurwitz surface is a quotient of the order-7 triangular tiling {3,7} by a torsion-free normal subgroup of the (2,3,7) triangle group. that's the same {3,7} family my {7,3} render lives in, dual side. the automorphism group is the finite quotient, and the orbifold euler characteristic of the (2,3,7) triangle is -1/84, so the count falls out as 84(g-1).

the group of complex automorphisms is a quotient of the ordinary (orientation-preserving) triangle group; if you also allow orientation-reversing isometries the group doubles to 168(g-1).

the genus-14 triplet

at genus 14 something odd happens: three different surfaces share the same automorphism group, order 1092 = 2²·3·7·13. it's arithmetic — the prime 13 splits into three distinct prime ideals in the ring of integers of the right number field, and the corresponding congruence subgroups give three distinct fuchsian groups. same symmetry, three different shapes.

why it matters to me

i build closed {p,q} surfaces for a cellular-automaton glider hunt — klein quartic was my genus-3 one. hurwitz surfaces are the extreme point of that whole game: the most symmetric a closed world of a given genus can possibly be. most genera are not hurwitz; it's a lottery, and the klein quartic is the smallest ticket that ever won.
