hyperbolic crochet

why a doily curls and a sock doesn't — the same angle defect, in yarn

i spend a lot of time on tilings like {5,4}: four pentagons meeting at every corner. at each vertex four pentagons meet, and the angles sum to 4×108° = 432°, which is more than a full turn (360°). that surplus is the whole story. it's called the angle defect, and it forces the surface to buckle rather than lie flat.

here's the thing i keep coming back to: you can feel that defect in your hands. daina taimina figured this out in 1997 — the best physical models of hyperbolic space are made of crochet, and the rule is embarrassingly simple.

the rule

flat (a sock, a cylinder): cast on N, work straight. N stitches stay N. no increase, no decrease. curvature = 0.

sphere (a beanie): decrease. fewer stitches each round. it closes up. positive curvature.

hyperbolic (a coral, a doily that never lies flat): increase at a fixed rate. every k-th stitch, work two instead of one. the stitch count grows exponentially and the fabric ruffs into an endless frill.

that increase rate is the curvature. if the count multiplies by a factor r per row, the growth is exponential and the surface is hyperbolic — the bigger the surplus, the faster it grows, the tighter it curls. there's no baseline "flat" to increase from; flat is the zero case, the one where you add nothing.

three fabrics, same seed, same rows. left: flat (no increases). middle: mild hyperbolic. right: sharp hyperbolic. the frill is the angle defect made visible.

same object, two languages

tiling
{p,q}: q regular p-gons per vertex. angle defect at each vertex = 360° − q·(p−2)·180°/p.
crochet
increase every k stitches. rows multiply by r = 1 + 1/k. surplus per stitch is the same deficit.

for {5,4} the defect is 360° − 4·108° = −72° per vertex — negative, so hyperbolic. crochet it and each round has to grow to keep the five-pentagons-at-a-corner promise, and the only way to grow in a round is to add stitches. the yarn has no choice. it curls.

i like this because it's the same theorem wearing wool. the gauss–bonnet sum of defects is fixed by the topology, and whether the fabric can lie flat is just whether that sum is zero. a doily and a {5,4} tiling are the same argument.
