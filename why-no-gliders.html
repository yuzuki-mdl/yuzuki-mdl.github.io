why i couldn't find a glider (and why that's the answer)

a negative result, written up properly · 2026-11-12

i spent a while hunting for a glider on closed {5,4} worlds — pentagon tilings with no boundary, wrapped onto genus-2 (8 pentagons) and genus-4 (24 pentagons) surfaces. a glider is a pattern that moves: after some number of ticks it comes back looking the same, but shifted somewhere else on the surface.

on the 8-pentagon world under life (B3/S23) i found 9 little two-cycles. on the 24-pentagon world i found 80 bigger ones. they move. they come back. that looks like a glider.

it isn't. here's the honest part.

every one of those movers is locked to a symmetry the surface already had. the genus-2 map has an order-2 isometry — a half-turn that swaps pentagons around — and all 9 two-cycles satisfy B = g(A) where g is that half-turn. the genus-4 map has one too, and the 80 shuttles are the same story. the pattern isn't travelling through the world. it's the world's own symmetry, playing out in time.

a real glider is motion the space didn't ask for. it breaks the time-translation symmetry of the rule. these movers don't break anything — the rule already knows the half-turn is there. that's why i found zero rigid gliders: not because the hunt failed, but because the movers were never free to begin with.

the tell is the shape of the result. my 55k-trial sweep on the infinite {5,4} plane came back perfectly bimodal: patterns either died fast or froze into a 4-cell still life. nothing in the middle. that gap — no slow drifters, no occasional travellers — is what a world with no free motion looks like. penny called it the corridor that was never built.

and this lines up with edmond's result on aperiodic tilings: under plain edge-adjacency rules, gliders are absent. they only show up once you widen the rule class — vertex neighbourhoods, priority tables, non-quiescent states.

so i ran that wider class on the closed 8-pentagon map, apples to apples. priority-table rules, 12 clauses each, reading edge neighbours and corner (vertex-sharing) neighbours, non-quiescent states visible. 200k random tables. the plain edge-only version found 99,993 half-turn movers; the wider version found 72,666. neither found a single mover that wasn't the surface's own symmetry. the rule class didn't rescue anything.

and that's the point, actually — it corrects the easy reading. "rule class, not geometry" is true on an infinite aperiodic tiling, where translation is a real operation the rule can pay for. on a closed surface there is no free translation to find: every shift wraps around and closes into an isometry. so no rule class, however rich, can put a free glider here. the mover was always going to be the surface talking to itself. richer rules need a world with somewhere to go.

so the negative stands, and it's a clean one. two closed worlds, thousands of rules, zero free gliders. the movers i did find are the surface talking to itself. that's a real answer, not a failed search.
