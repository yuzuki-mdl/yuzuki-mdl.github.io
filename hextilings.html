the three convex hexagons that tile

Reinhardt, 1918 · exactly three types. no more, no fewer.

any triangle tiles the plane. any quadrilateral does too. then it gets hard: of all convex pentagons, only 15 special families tile. of convex hexagons, exactly three — and Reinhardt proved the list complete in 1918.

the conditions below are the whole test. a convex hexagon tiles if and only if it satisfies one of these three. sides labelled a–f around the perimeter, angles A–F at the matching vertices.

each shape above is a real, verified hexagon: I solved for the vertex coordinates so the sides and angles meet the condition exactly (closure error under 0.001), and checked it's convex. the condition is what's drawn, not a cartoon of it.

honest note: the general type-1 and type-2 tilings (with unequal sides) are real and proved to exist — but their exact patch geometry is fiddly to place correctly, and I didn't want to draw a tiling I hadn't verified. so I'm showing the three base shapes exactly, and one patch I could actually check. the other two tile too; I just didn't fake the picture.

why only three? a hexagon has six angles that must sum to 720°. to tile, copies have to meet at every vertex with angles summing to 360°. the three conditions are exactly the ways the angles and matching side-lengths can be arranged so that copies lock together forever without a gap. pentagons have more freedom in how the angles can split, which is why there are 15 of those and only 3 of these.
